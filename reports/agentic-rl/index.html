Full reading outline and research questions

1. Understand the interaction loop

Build a concrete picture of what an agent observes, generates, and changes before diving into the training objective.

  • The Landscape: §1 — Introduction
  • Search-R1: §1 — Introduction
  • Search-R1: §3.2–3.3 — Multi-turn generation and the training template
  • The Landscape: §2.1–2.4 — MDPs, environment state, action space, and transitions
  1. What is hidden in the environment state, and what does the model actually receive as an observation? When is the interaction genuinely partially observable?
  2. In a two-search trajectory, which tokens come from the policy and which come from the environment? How do model calls, environment steps, and episodes differ?
  3. What would change if a tool returned noisy or incomplete results? Which parts of the interaction model would need to change?

2. Connect outcomes to learning

Connect trajectory probabilities, returns, and expected reward. Understand why a non-differentiable tool can still sit inside a trainable interaction loop.

  • The Landscape: §2.5–2.6 — Reward functions and learning objectives
  • Search-R1: §3.4 — Reward modeling
  • Search-R1: Appendix A — Formalizing RL with a search engine
  1. Why can policy gradient improve a search query without differentiating through the search engine? Which factors of trajectory probability depend on the policy parameters?
  2. If the only reward arrives after the final answer, what evidence does the update provide about an earlier query? What remains ambiguous about that query’s contribution?
  3. How would a cost for tool calls change the objective? When might optimizing answer correctness alone encourage an inefficient strategy?

3. Read the actual policy update

Map the abstract objective onto PPO and GRPO, then identify exactly which parts of an interleaved trajectory contribute to the policy loss.

  • The Landscape: §2.7 — Read REINFORCE, PPO, and GRPO; skim DPO for context
  • Search-R1: §3.1 — RL with a search engine, including retrieved-token masking
  • Search-R1: §5.1 — PPO versus GRPO
  • Search-R1: §5.4 — Retrieved-token masking analysis
  1. What baseline defines the advantage in PPO versus GRPO? What useful signal disappears when every rollout in a group receives the same reward?
  2. What do the old/new policy ratio, clipping, and reference-policy KL each control? Which of these mechanisms are distinct?
  3. Why are retrieved tokens excluded from the policy-action loss while remaining available as context? What would go wrong if you treated them as sampled policy actions?

4. Separate evidence from interpretation

Learn to read agent benchmarks as controlled experiments, with explicit attention to the initial model, retrieval environment, and inference budget.

  • Search-R1: §4 — Datasets, baselines, setup, and performance
  • Search-R1: §5.2–5.3 — Initialization, response length, and valid searches
  • Agent Lightning: §4.1 — Search-agent experiment
  • The Landscape: §4.1 — Search and research agents
  1. Which comparisons isolate the effect of RL, and which also change the initial model, retriever, or interaction budget? What additional control would strengthen the claim?
  2. What can search count and response length tell you about strategy quality? What plausible alternative explanations would you need to rule out?
  3. What experiment would distinguish benchmark-specific adaptation from a more general ability to decide when and how to search?

5. Move beyond a single growing transcript

Understand how a deployment harness shapes context, tools, and control flow—and why the training data must preserve the conditions under which actions were sampled.

  • Agent Lightning: §1 — Introduction and the harnessed agentic RL formulation
  • Agent Lightning: §2.1 — Retokenization and sample merging
  • The Landscape: §3.3 — Memory
  1. What belongs to the policy model, the harness, and the external environment? How does summarizing the history change what the policy can observe?
  2. Why can identical text have different token boundaries after retokenization? What does a policy update need to preserve about the original sampled action?
  3. If a harness summarizes context or spawns a subagent, when is it valid to merge model calls into a single training sequence? What information could be lost?

6. Follow credit across samples

Keep task-level rollouts, training samples, and individual tokens distinct. Examine how sample construction changes the estimator and the effective weight of each trajectory.

  • Search-R1: §3.1 · revisit — The GRPO objective
  • Agent Lightning: §2.2–2.3 — Advantage calculation and loss normalization
  • Agent Lightning: §4.3.3 — Training dynamics and estimator comparisons
  1. A successful rollout becomes three samples; a failed rollout remains one. How do rollout-level and sample-level reward baselines differ, even though the task outcomes are unchanged?
  2. How do token-mean, sample-level, and rollout-level normalization weight short versus long trajectories? Which weighting matches the objective you intend to optimize?
  3. Could differences attributed to a new RL algorithm actually arise from sample construction or normalization? What ablation would isolate the cause?

7. Audit what success means

Distinguish training reward, benchmark success, and the user’s actual goal. Study how data construction and verification constrain what the agent learns.

  • The Landscape: §4.2.3 — RL for automated software engineering
  • Agent Lightning: §4.3.1–4.3.2 — Dataset filtering and preventing reward hacking
  • Agent Lightning: §4.2 — General instruction-following agent
  • The Landscape: §6.1 — Trustworthiness
  1. Which behaviors could improve the measured reward without fixing the underlying issue? What parts of the verifier or execution environment make those shortcuts possible?
  2. Which examples are removed by preprocessing and filtering? How might that selection alter the task distribution and the behavior you learn?
  3. What does passing the final tests establish—and what does it leave unresolved about robustness, intermediate reasoning, or performance on unseen tasks?

8. Assemble the learning system

Place rollout generation, tool execution, reward computation, and parameter updates in one architecture. Understand where correctness and throughput can come apart.

  • The Landscape: §5.1–5.2 — Environment simulators and RL frameworks
  • Agent Lightning: §2.4 — Training backend complexity
  • Agent Lightning: §3 — System design; focus on §3.1–3.2
  • The Landscape: §6.2–6.3 — Scaling training and environments
  1. Where do model inference, tool execution, reward computation, and optimization run? Which bottleneck determines throughput in different workloads?
  2. What changes when rollouts and updates run asynchronously? How would you measure the age of the policy that generated each training sample?
  3. Why do retries, duplicate API calls, and variable sample counts matter for the training data—not just service reliability? What should the monitoring distinguish?

9. Build your own account of the field

Connect planning, tool use, memory, and self-improvement to concrete training mechanisms. Develop a defensible explanation you can teach and a question you could investigate.

  • The Landscape: §3.1–3.2 — Planning and tool use
  • The Landscape: §3.4–3.5 — Self-improvement and reasoning
  • The Landscape: §6.4 — The mechanistic debate on RL in LLMs
  • Search-R1: §6 — Conclusions
  • Agent Lightning: §6 — Conclusion
  • The Landscape: §7 — Conclusion
  1. Which capabilities are directly supported by the experiments you read, and which are broader interpretations? What evidence would justify the stronger interpretation?
  2. How do weight updates, in-context adaptation, harness changes, and increased inference compute produce different kinds of improvement?
  3. Which unresolved problem now seems most tractable: credit assignment, training collapse, environment quality, transfer, or online feedback? What is the smallest experiment that could falsify your hypothesis?
